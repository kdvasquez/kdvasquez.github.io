Looking back, I don't think I fully appreciated how much I grew in the past year because I did not stop to write any of it down.

Between internship work, tutoring, portfolio projects, and the looming pressure of full-time job applications, the months blurred together.

So this is my attempt to start documenting it.

---

After graduating USC, I felt more sad than happy about the accomplishment that is graduation. I was a new grad with no job offer and no internship experience. I had many projects, but no clarity in what branch I wanted to merge into (no pun intended).

Like many new graduates entering tech in 2025, I quickly learned that the transition from student to engineer is less of a straight line and more of a series of pivots, unexpected opportunities, and continuous learning.

I was internally shouting for an opportunity, whatever it be, to help me find clarity, and somehow, it did. Someone close to me connected me to the opportunity that I genuinely consider a blessing: my first internship at a Peruvian startup called Platia.

As someone with roots in Peru, there was something meaningful about my first professional engineering work contributing to technology being built for communities in my home country. Platia's mission is to expand digital payments and financial services to underserved and rural communities in Peru while navigating the realities of financial regulation, changing markets, and rapidly evolving technology. It was my first glimpse into fintech, and banking solutions in Peru work quite differently from the United States, I soon found out.

For the first half of my time there, I worked on modernizing internal administrative tools, helping migrate an aging Angular 16 codebase toward Angular 19. I had never worked professionally with Angular before, so a large part of my job initially involved learning the framework itself. I was given the time and trust to study the documentation, take courses, and start essentially from scratch following a new design on Figma. It was also AI’s peak, so I was fascinated how the days of trying to position a component or customize it the right way  were long gone as it could be generated instantly. Although I had started with ChatGPT, Claude was my first trusted bestie for the ride (win for design).

Overtime, my responsibilities ranged from:

- Frontend Design implementation
- Integrating and testing authentication flows and payment systems.
- Documenting codebase ReadMe’s
- Researching frontend frameworks, plugins, and tech stacks

## Learning how engineering teams communicate

One of the biggest surprises had very little to do with programming.

I learned how important communication is.

I learned to verify my work before escalating issues, gather evidence before asking questions, and communicate clearly enough that teammates could quickly understand what I had already tested and where I thought the problem might be.

As someone naturally quieter in team settings, this was challenging, and even more so when the primary language was Spanish. I am fluent, but given how a lot of communication is done through messaging, I found myself having to input my draft into Chat to help it be nice and clean with all the accents properly added, and get my inquiry across.

There were many moments where I worried that I wasn't explaining technical problems clearly enough, especially when debugging issues that lived somewhere between the frontend and backend.

A 401 Unauthorized error might be authentication.

A 500 Internal Server Error might be backend.

Sometimes it was the frontend after all.

I felt bad whenever I had to say it the problem wasn’t on my end (silent blame game between frontend and backend). But learning how to narrow down possibilities and communicate findings and set aside my overthinking became just as important as writing code.

## Learning how software gets built outside the classroom

School projects taught me how to program.

Professional work taught me how software actually gets shipped.

I learned Git workflows, development versus production environments, API contracts, authentication systems, and the realities of integrating with services that are still actively being developed.

Browser developer tools quickly became some of my most-used tools:

- Network tabs
- Console logs
- API responses
- HTTP status codes

I became very familiar with 401s, 403s, 404s, and 500s.

I learned to work with REST APIs through tools like APIdog and gradually became more comfortable tracing problems through requests, parameters, tokens, and response payloads.

## Learning alongside AI

Another interesting part of entering the industry in 2025 is that learning how to use AI effectively became part of the job itself.

I experimented with different models and tools, each with different strengths and weaknesses depending on the task.

More importantly, I learned that AI works best when paired with engineering judgment.

Sometimes the correct move was asking better questions.

Sometimes it was reading documentation.

Sometimes it was stepping away from the computer entirely and returning later with fresh eyes.

## Learning about fintech in Peru

Before Platia, I had never spent much time thinking about how payment systems actually work.

Working on products designed for underserved communities gave me a new appreciation for how infrastructure shapes opportunity.

Features that feel ordinary in major cities can still be inaccessible in rural areas (e.g banks charge a lot for the usage of credit cards).

Building financial tools for those environments means balancing usability, trust, regulation, security, and business realities all at once.

It was my first exposure to fintech, and it made me appreciate how much engineering exists beyond the code itself.

---

To balance remote work — and because I was admittedly getting restless spending all day indoors — I also picked up a part-time position as an after-school tutor through HeyTutor.

I taught elementary students across multiple grade levels, adapting lessons to students who learned at very different speeds and in very different ways.

Surprisingly, tutoring strengthened many of the same skills I was developing as an engineer.

Teaching forced me to explain ideas clearly.

To adjust explanations when something wasn't working.

To think quickly.

To communicate with intention.

If a concept was too advanced for a third grader, I had to find another way to explain it.

Software engineering often feels surprisingly similar (the concept is too advanced for me and I ask Chat to explain it to me like a baby).

Teaching also gave me back a confidence that I didn't realize I had lost during the uncertainty of the new graduate job search.

I became more comfortable speaking, leading groups, managing classrooms, and balancing kindness with authority.

I worked with school administrators, program coordinators, and fellow tutors, including reconnecting with classmates (who were my colleagues at the school I tutored at) I hadn't seen since childhood.

By the end of the school year, saying goodbye to my students was harder than I expected.

---

If my first year after graduation taught me anything, it's that growth rarely looks dramatic while it's happening.

It looks like debugging authentication issues for three hours.

It looks like translating technical questions into another language.

It looks like learning an unfamiliar framework one week and presenting a feature demo the next.

It looks like helping a fourth grader finally understand fractions after twenty minutes of trying different explanations.

None of it looked quite like what I imagined when I graduated.

But looking back, I think it was exactly the kind of year I needed.
